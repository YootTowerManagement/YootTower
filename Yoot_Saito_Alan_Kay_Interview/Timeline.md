# Timeline

This timeline follows the computing history discussed in Yoot Saito's Alan Kay interview, Alan Kay's "Early History of Smalltalk," and the restored appendices.

### 1951-1953
[Whirlwind](Technologies.md#whirlwind-computer), developed at MIT, demonstrates real-time interaction and display-oriented computing. Kay later cites it as part of the lineage behind interactive personal computing.

### 1959-1963
Several early systems establish ideas that later matter to object-oriented programming and personal computing, including interactive systems, protection mechanisms, and higher-level machine architectures.

### 1960-1964
Early work on classes, instances, coroutines, and protected execution contributes to the conceptual background for [Simula](Technologies.md#simula), [Smalltalk](Technologies.md#smalltalk), and data abstraction.

### 1961
Kay works with an Air Force file-management system on the Burroughs 220. He later describes this as an early encounter with modular interfaces, wrapping, and representation independence.

The [Burroughs B5000](Technologies.md#b5000) appears with a stack architecture and high-level-language orientation. Bob Barton's design becomes one of Kay's important influences.

### 1962
[Wes Clark](People.md#wesley-a-clark-uc-berkeley-linc-mit-mit-lincoln-laboratory-washington-university-clark-rockoff-and-associates) develops the [LINC](Technologies.md#linc-laboratory-instrument-computer), one of the earliest personal computers. Kay points to the LINC as an important precedent because biomedical researchers assembled and used their own machines.

### 1963
Melvin Conway introduces software coroutines, a control-flow idea that influences [Simula](Technologies.md#simula) and later thinking about processes and objects.

### 1964
Tony Hoare introduces record-class ideas that become part of the background for [Simula](Technologies.md#simula).

### 1965
[Gordon Moore](People.md#gordon-moore-uc-berkeley-cit-intel) publishes the observation later called Moore's Law, shaping expectations for rapid improvements in integrated circuits.

[Simula](Technologies.md#simula) begins to take shape from ALGOL-derived simulation work, incorporating coroutines and class-like structures.

### 1966
Kay enters graduate school at the [University of Utah](Institutions.md#university-of-utah) computing environment and encounters ARPA-funded work, including [Ivan Sutherland](People.md#ivan-sutherland-university-of-utah-cmu-harvard-portland-state-university-cit-mit-arpa-evans-sutherland)'s [Sketchpad](Technologies.md#sketchpad).

Kay studies [Simula](Technologies.md#simula), whose objects, classes, and simulation processes become central influences on his later design of [Smalltalk](Technologies.md#smalltalk).

### 1967
Kay begins work with [Ed Cheadle](People.md#ed-cheadle-flex-machine-project-memcor-montec-corporation-university-of-utah) on the [Flex Machine](Technologies.md#flex-machine), an early personal-computer project intended to support dynamic simulation and high-level end-user programming.

LOGO development begins in the MIT orbit around [Seymour Papert](People.md#seymour-papert-mit-university-of-geneva-university-of-paris-national-physical-laboratory), connecting programming languages with children's learning.

### 1967-1969
The Flex Machine and its language explore object references, extensible interpretation, windows, and user-level modeling. These ideas feed directly into Kay's later Dynabook and Smalltalk work.

Kay encounters systems including [GRAIL](Technologies.md#grail), LOGO, and [PLANNER](Technologies.md#planner), strengthening his interest in graphical interaction, end-user authoring, and pattern-directed computation.

### 1968
[Douglas Engelbart](People.md#douglas-engelbart-oregon-state-university-uc-berkeley-sri-tymshare-mcdonnell-douglas-bootstrap-institute-the-douglas-engelbart-institute) visits Utah and demonstrates ideas from [NLS](Technologies.md#nls-on-line-system). The encounter strongly affects Kay's vision of interactive computing.

R. S. Barton gives his influential Alta Ski Lodge talk on computers as communication devices, contributing to Kay's conceptual foundation for object-oriented personal computing.

Engelbart's public [Mother of All Demos](Technologies.md#the-mother-of-all-demos) shows the mouse, hypertext, outlining, collaboration, and interactive display work.

### 1969
Kay continues developing the Flex Machine ideas and absorbs work on [PLANNER](Technologies.md#planner), early LOGO, [GRAIL](Technologies.md#grail), and capability-based systems.

[CAL-TSS](Technologies.md#cal-tss) and related capability-system work influence Kay's thinking about protection, object references, and kernel elegance.

### 1970
Xerox decides to establish the [Palo Alto Research Center](Institutions.md#xerox-parc-palo-alto-research-center), led by George Pake.

Dave Fisher develops the Control Definition Language, which later influences Smalltalk interpreter design and extensible control structures.

### July 1970
Xerox PARC is formally established.

### September 1970
[Bob Taylor](People.md#bob-taylor-southern-methodist-university-university-of-texas-xerox-parc-arpa-dec) is recruited to lead PARC's Computer Science Laboratory.

### 1971
Kay's group begins developing the KiddiKomp and miniCOM ideas, early steps toward child-centered personal computing and the [Dynabook](Technologies.md#dynabook).

Chuck Thacker and Ed McCreight begin work on the Alto design at PARC, creating the machine that becomes the main platform for early Smalltalk work.

### 1971-1972
[Chris Jeffers](People.md#chris-jeffers-xerox-parc) is part of the Learning Research Group during the early Smalltalk period.

### January 1972
Work begins on "The Reading Machine," a PARC effort involving Butler Lampson, Chuck Thacker, Bill English, Jerry Elkind, and George Pake that addresses custom chips, displays, and personal-computer design issues.

### May 15, 1972
Kay sends the "KiddiKomputer" memo to Butler Lampson, Chuck Thacker, [Bill English](People.md#bill-english-xerox-parc-sri-sun-microsystems), Jerry Elkind, and George Pake. The memo describes a multifunctional personal computer for children and adults.

### Summer-Fall 1972
[Dan Ingalls](People.md#dan-ingalls-harvard-stanford-university-xerox-parc-apple-inc-interval-research-corporation), Henry Fuchs, Ted Kaehler, John Shoch, Steve Purcell, Bob Shur, Bonny Tennenbaum, and [Barbara Deutsch](People.md#barbara-deutsch-xerox-parc) contribute to the discussions and implementation work that shape early Smalltalk.

### September 1972
Kay, Butler Lampson, and Chuck Thacker discuss building a machine suited to their personal-computing vision, leading to the bet around what becomes the Alto and the KiddiKomp direction.

### Late 1972
[Smalltalk-72](Technologies.md#smalltalk-72) begins after Kay challenges himself and the group to define a powerful language in a page of code.

### November 1972
Kay gives a talk on Smalltalk at the MIT AI Lab, discussing process structure, properties, messages, and operators. The talk influences [Carl Hewitt](People.md#carl-hewitt-mit-ai-lab-keio-university-stanford-university)'s Actor model work.

### 1973
The first interim Dynabook, "Bilbo," appears at PARC and shows the famous bitmap picture of Cookie Monster.

PARC introduces overlapping windows and continues developing graphical user-interface conventions in the Smalltalk environment.

Hewitt and collaborators publish the formal Actor model of computation, connecting message-passing ideas with AI and concurrent computation.

### 1973-1975
Smalltalk documentation, demonstrations, videotapes, and applications expand. The system becomes a practical multimedia and interactive programming environment rather than only a language experiment.

### 1974
Smalltalk work focuses heavily on user-interface ideas, including overlapping windows, text editing, painting, animation, music, and multimedia documents.

### 1974-1976
Smalltalk evolves through Smalltalk-72 and Smalltalk-74 toward [Smalltalk-76](Technologies.md#smalltalk-76), adding performance improvements and a more structured object system.

### 1975
A publication blackout lifts, allowing broader discussion and promotion of Smalltalk research.

The group reevaluates Smalltalk-72 and Smalltalk-74, setting up the transition toward a more modern Smalltalk architecture.

### January 1976
The "Let's Burn Our Disk Packs" meeting at Pajaro Dunes marks a major reset in thinking about Smalltalk's future direction.

### Late 1976
The transition toward [Smalltalk-76](Technologies.md#smalltalk-76) introduces more explicit class structure, inheritance machinery, bytecodes, and improved memory management.

### 1977-1979
Smalltalk-76 development continues, with more documentation, implementation work, and use in research applications.

### 1979-1981
Smalltalk gains wider recognition outside the Learning Research Group through publications, demonstrations, and influence on education, programming languages, and graphical computing.

### 1980-1983
[Smalltalk-80](Technologies.md#smalltalk) is released outside Xerox, making the language and virtual machine more widely available and establishing the version that becomes the basis for books, ports, and commercial implementations.
